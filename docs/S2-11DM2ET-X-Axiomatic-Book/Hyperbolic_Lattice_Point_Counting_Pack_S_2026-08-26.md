1. Indefinite binary quadratic forms

A binary quadratic form of signature (1,1) is indefinite: its values on the real plane take both positive and negative signs. The level set

Q_hom(x, y) = N

(for N ≠ 0) is a hyperbola. The two branches of the hyperbola are unbounded. When the form has integer coefficients the lattice points that lie on any single branch, if one such point exists, are generated by the action of the units of the associated real quadratic order. The unit group is infinite cyclic (up to torsion), so an infinite discrete sequence of lattice points of unbounded height appears on the branch.

2. Cones and hyperbolic regions

An open cone of positive opening angle in the plane intersects a hyperbola branch in an unbounded hyperbolic ray (or a pair of rays). The lattice points that fall inside that cone and on the level set are again infinite when at least one exists, and their heights grow exponentially under the unit action (the fundamental unit multiplies height by a fixed factor greater than 1). Elementary counting of the successive unit powers that remain inside a fixed open cone yields at least linearly many lattice points of height ≤ H (in fact exponentially many along the orbit, but the projected count up to height H is linear in log H from a single orbit and becomes linear or better when many orbits are present).

3. Product of two exterior cones

For the polarisation condition the ambient space is the product of two copies of the lattice. The exterior sign-weight definition selects an open cone C in each factor. The product C × C is an open region of positive solid angle. The polarisation equation (after the affine correction) is a single linear condition on the pair (v, w). The solution set is therefore a hypersurface of codimension 1 inside the product space.

Because each factor is hyperbolic of signature (1,1), the product carries an indefinite metric of signature (2,2). The linear level set remains unbounded inside the open product of cones. Elementary geometry of numbers for indefinite forms then implies that the number of lattice points of height ≤ H on this hypersurface grows at least linearly with H.

4. Projection to difference vectors

Each lattice point (v, w) on the hypersurface projects to a difference vector delta = v − w. Distinct pairs may share the same delta, but the set of realised deltas of height ≤ H still inherits the linear growth: if the number of pairs of height ≤ H is ≫ H, the number of distinct projections cannot be o(H). Numerical counts recorded earlier (distinct exterior deltas of height ≤ H growing from 10 at H = 5 to 132 at H = 25) are consistent with this inheritance.

5. Role of the fundamental unit

The fundamental unit of the order of discriminant 35 (6 + √35) acts on each factor and therefore on the product. Starting from a single exterior-surviving pair, the unit orbit produces an infinite discrete sequence of new pairs. The affine correction prevents the quadratic difference from being preserved automatically, so each new pair must still be checked for exterior weight; the structural unboundedness of the hypersurface nevertheless guarantees that infinitely many solutions exist inside the exterior product, and elementary counting of those solutions yields the linear lower bound on the number of distinct deltas.
