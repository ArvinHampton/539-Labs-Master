1. Historical Discussion: The Arc of Gravitational Inquiry
Gravity's narrative begins in antiquity, predating formal theories. Presocratic atomists like
Democritus (c. 460–370 BCE) posited gravity as a downward tendency of atoms, an emergent
order from void chaos. Aristotle (384–322 BCE) formalized this in Physics, viewing gravity as
elemental striving: earth seeks the cosmic center, fire the periphery—a teleological "natural
place" without mathematical rigor.

Medieval scholars, including Al-Biruni (973–1048 CE) and Ibn Sina (Avicenna, 980–1037 CE),
refined these ideas, measuring Earth's density and debating infinite universes, yet gravity
remained qualitative. The Renaissance pivot came with Copernicus (1473–1543 CE) and
Galileo (1564–1642 CE), who demonstrated uniform acceleration of falling bodies (g≈9.8g
\approx 9.8g≈9.8 m/s²), challenging Aristotelian hierarchies.
Isaac Newton's Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687) revolutionized this: gravity
as a universal force, unifying terrestrial falls and celestial orbits. Inspired by Kepler's laws
(1609–1619)—elliptical orbits, equal areas, T2∝a3T^2 \propto a^3T2∝a3—Newton inferred an
inverse-square law from centripetal force requirements for circular motion. This "first great
unification" explained planetary perturbations, tides, and comets, yet Newton himself demurred
on mechanism: "hypotheses non fingo" (I feign no hypotheses).
The 19th century exposed cracks: Uranus's orbital anomalies led to Neptune's discovery (1846),
validating Newton, but Mercury's perihelion precession (43 arcsec/century excess) resisted.
Urbain Le Verrier's Vulcan hypothesis failed. Meanwhile, Maxwell's electromagnetism (1865)
introduced fields, hinting at mediated forces.
Albert Einstein's special relativity (1905) abolished absolute space-time, mandating gravity's
reformulation. From 1907's equivalence principle—accelerating frames mimic gravity—to 1915's
general relativity, Einstein recast gravity as spacetime curvature. Published November 25, 1915,
the field equations resolved Mercury's anomaly, predicted light bending (confirmed 1919
Eddington eclipse), and birthed black holes, gravitational waves (detected 2015 LIGO).
